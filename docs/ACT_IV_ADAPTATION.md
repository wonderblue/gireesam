# Act IV compact episode: source and adaptation notes

## Witness and scope

This episode uses the Andhra Bharati transcription of the **1909 second-edition first printing** of *Kanyasulkam*, identified in the [text index](https://andhrabharati.com/nATakamulu/kanyASulkamu/index.html). The selected [Act IV scene 1](https://andhrabharati.com/nATakamulu/kanyASulkamu/41.html), [scene 2](https://andhrabharati.com/nATakamulu/kanyASulkamu/42.html), [scene 3](https://andhrabharati.com/nATakamulu/kanyASulkamu/43.html), [scene 4](https://andhrabharati.com/nATakamulu/kanyASulkamu/44.html), and [scene 5](https://andhrabharati.com/nATakamulu/kanyASulkamu/45.html) supply the episode's source sequence and plot facts. This is distinct from the **1961-based witness used for the Act I Telugu UI**; wording, scene divisions, and page references are not interchangeable.

This is one compact playable episode, not a complete Act IV or full-play adaptation. It uses three consecutive stops: Ramappantulu's house, Lubdhavadhani's wedding setting, and Agnihotravadhani's residence.

## Text facts used

These are plot facts from the selected 1909 witness, not new game mechanics:

- **Scene 1, Ramappantulu's house:** Ramappantulu tells Madhuravani privately that he wrote a letter in Agnihotravadhani's name to withdraw the proposed match. Lubdhavadhani confronts him with the letter; Ramappantulu denies authorship in that confrontation. Madhuravani improvises as the dispute develops. The letter's claim is not itself confirmation from its named sender.
- **Scenes 2–4, Lubdhavadhani's house and wedding setting:** Lubdhavadhani and the other men bargain over ritual custom, timing, ceremony expense, and public standing. The farce turns on status and respectable male authority as the wedding proceeds around the men's competing claims.
- **Scene 5, Agnihotravadhani's residence:** Gireesam teaches Venkatesam while privately pressuring Buchchamma to elope with him. He frames elopement as the only way to stop her sister's arranged marriage and uses emotional pressure, including threats of self-harm.

## Adaptation choices

The route uses a silent observer rather than making the player speak as Madhuravani or Buchchamma. At the first stop, one new evidence interaction asks the player to verify the named sender, delay a decision, or seek help. Those options are adaptation mechanics, not choices found in the play. Each is a cautious response to uncertainty; none supplies independent confirmation from the named sender, forces disclosure, decides the wedding, or changes the source plot.

The second stop compresses the wedding scenes to expose bargaining over ritual, cost, status, and men's respectability. Humor targets the bargaining and competing performances of authority, not a woman being sold or controlled. The final stop shows Gireesam's lesson as cover for a coercive pitch. His ultimatum is not framed as romance or as an effective rescue plan; Buchchamma's concern for her sister does not make her responsible for the crisis, and the player cannot accept the elopement on her behalf.

All game text is original English adaptation, not translation or quotation. No Telugu dialogue, transliteration, or machine-translated line is added. The standard three-stage escape campaign and Acts I–III story routes remain separate.

## Contributor safeguards

- Keep the 1909 second-edition first-printing witness separate from the 1961-based witness used for the Act I Telugu UI; verify the chosen witness before adding any quotation.
- Do not add Telugu dialogue or transliteration without exact wording verified against the selected edition scan and review by a fluent reader.
- Keep the letter's author, named sender, holder, and witnesses distinct. A claim in a document is not proof of its authorship.
- Keep the wedding satire on bargaining, status, and male respectability. Never use Subbi as a prize, collectible, object, or player objective.
- Keep Madhuravani's improvisation hers. Do not turn the player's route through her into a triumph over the scheme.
- Keep Buchchamma's concern hers; never blame her for the marriage crisis or reward a pressured elopement as romance.
